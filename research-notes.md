# TradeWise Academy — Curriculum Evidence Base

The curriculum is structured as education rather than investment advice. The sequence begins with market mechanics and risk before charting or strategy selection. This choice reflects the SEC’s explanation that all investments carry risk, including potential loss, and its descriptions of business, volatility, liquidity, inflation, and interest-rate risk. The curriculum explicitly separates a simulated environment from a brokerage account and avoids promises of performance.[1]

Day-trading lessons carry heightened, plain-language caution. FINRA states that day trading can be extremely risky, may be inappropriate for people with limited resources or experience, and can involve losses beyond the original investment when margin or short selling is used.[2] The app’s first release therefore contains neither margin nor short-sale mechanics in its paper account. It teaches these concepts only as risk awareness.

Options are presented as a capped introductory module, after risk and position sizing. The Options Institute describes its introductory curriculum as covering puts, calls, potential benefits, and risks; its later materials use payoff diagrams and address spread strategies.[3] TradeWise Academy confines its first option lessons to vocabulary and payoff awareness, not trade recommendations.

## Glossary and Simulation Expansion

The glossary uses original plain-language educational definitions and organizes terms into market mechanics, analysis, risk, orders, and options. The SEC’s Investor.gov glossary demonstrates the breadth of terms needed for investor education, including bid/ask concepts, order types, short selling, market structure, and disclosure vocabulary.[4] Options terminology is framed as introductory vocabulary only; the Options Industry Council reference library separates foundations, pricing, volatility, and advanced Greeks, reinforcing that option terms require contextual risk education.[5]

The expanded Market Lab uses only hand-authored **synthetic scenarios**. Its symbols, price paths, catalyst cards, and scenario labels do not source, reproduce, or imply current quotes, forecasts, or real-market performance. The scenarios are named by market regime—such as trend continuation, risk-off pullback, and range compression—so learners can practice structured planning without treating the visual as a real-time signal.

## Financial Systems and Market Structure Expansion

The financial-structure curriculum treats company reporting and market plumbing as connected systems. The SEC’s guide explains that balance sheets show assets, liabilities, and shareholder equity at a point in time; income and cash-flow statements describe activity over periods; and a statement of shareholders’ equity shows changes in owners’ interests.[6] The accompanying lessons emphasize interpretation and questions, not the selection of any security.

The market-structure track distinguishes the different roles that interact around a trade. Investor.gov describes broker-dealers, clearing agencies, exchanges, alternative trading systems, investment advisers, self-regulatory organizations, and transfer agents as separate market participants with different functions.[7] The app uses this structure to make execution, clearing, settlement, and custody concepts easier to connect.

## Brokerage Costs, Short Selling, and Monetary Policy

The account and execution track treats costs as part of the structure of investing rather than a footnote. FINRA groups common investor costs into transaction costs, advisory fees, and ongoing expenses, and cautions that zero-commission trades do not necessarily mean zero investing costs.[8] The library therefore includes clear definitions of spreads, markups, fund expenses, margin interest, and other fee concepts without recommending an account or provider.

Short selling is taught as a limited risk-awareness topic. FINRA describes a short sale as selling stock an investor does not own, notes that it is conducted through a margin account, and distinguishes reported short interest from short-sale volume.[9] The TradeWise paper account remains cash-only and does not enable shorting or leverage.

Macro lessons distinguish monetary policy from market prediction. The Federal Reserve describes monetary policy as the central bank’s approach to influencing short-term rates and overall financial conditions in pursuit of its statutory goals. The course treats policy as broad context, not a trade signal.[10]

## Futures and Leveraged Derivatives Boundary

The derivatives reference track treats futures, commodity options, foreign exchange products, and other leveraged contracts as advanced-risk topics. The CFTC’s customer-education office cautions that such products can be complex and highly leveraged, with margin and time-limited contracts capable of amplifying gains, losses, and margin-call risk.[11] TradeWise Academy therefore explains contract vocabulary, risk structures, and fraud awareness but does not model leverage, futures, foreign exchange, crypto assets, or real derivative trades in its local simulator.

## Funds, Corporate Events, and Investor Protection

The expanded product map distinguishes an operating company’s stock from pooled vehicles and other wrappers. Investor.gov explains that registered ETFs pool investors’ money into portfolios and trade on exchanges; fund shares can trade at market prices that differ from net asset value, and fees and underlying holdings remain central to risk.[12] Its REIT overview distinguishes exchange-traded and non-traded structures and highlights liquidity, valuation transparency, distribution-source, and conflict considerations for non-traded REITs.[13]

The public-company research track introduces EDGAR as a free database for company and fund filings, with 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K reports positioned as different types of disclosed information rather than trading signals.[14] Corporate-action lessons cover splits, dividends, rights offerings, mergers, and liquidations. FINRA notes that a stock split changes share count but not an owner’s proportional equity, while liquidations place common shareholders behind creditors in claims on proceeds.[15]

The investor-protection track teaches independent research, source verification, and fraud red flags. Investor.gov cautions against using unsolicited messages and press releases as the sole basis for an investment decision and flags claims of extraordinary or guaranteed returns as warning signs.[16]
