# Technical Analysis Curriculum Research Notes

## Scope and source framing

The expanded curriculum will teach technical analysis as a **structured way to observe price, volume, and related market information**, not as a predictive system or a recommendation engine. The CFTC’s glossary describes charting as using graphs and charts to plot price trends, average movements, volume, and open interest; it also notes that glossary definitions do not suggest a trading strategy or economic theory. This supports an observation-first, limitation-aware learning design.[1]

CME Group’s technical-analysis course groups the subject into chart types, moving averages, trend and continuation patterns, reversal patterns, support and resistance, Fibonacci retracements and extensions, oscillators including MACD/RSI/Stochastics, and the relationship between technical and fundamental analysis.[2]

## Design decisions

| Curriculum lane | Teaching emphasis | Required limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Chart construction | Timeframe, OHLC data, candlesticks, range, gaps | A chart is a record of selected past observations, not an outcome forecast. |
| Market structure | Trend, swing points, support/resistance, ranges, breakouts | Levels are areas of interest; apparent breaks can fail. |
| Trend and momentum tools | Moving averages, MACD, RSI, Stochastic, rate of change | Settings and lookback periods change outputs; derived indicators lag their inputs. |
| Volume and participation | Relative volume, volume confirmation, volume-price relationship | Reported volume alone does not reveal intent or guarantee follow-through. |
| Pattern vocabulary | Continuation, reversal, channels, triangles, flags, head-and-shoulders | A named pattern is a hypothesis requiring context, invalidation, and risk planning. |
| Process integration | Multi-timeframe review, risk boundary, study journal, technical vs fundamental inputs | A technical observation cannot substitute for liquidity, company research, or risk management. |
